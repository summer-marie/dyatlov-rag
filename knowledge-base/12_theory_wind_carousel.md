# Theory: Wind Carousel / Katabatic Wind

## What It Proposes

In 2019, a Swedish–Russian expedition was made to the site, and investigators ultimately proposed that a violent katabatic wind was a plausible explanation for the incident. Katabatic winds are somewhat rare events and can be extremely violent. They were implicated in a 1978 case at Anaris Mountain in Sweden, where eight hikers were killed and one was severely injured. The topography of these locations was noted to be similar, according to the expedition.

A sudden katabatic wind would have made it impossible to remain in the tent, and the most rational course of action would have been for the hikers to cover the tent with snow and seek shelter behind the tree line. On top of the tent, a flashlight also was left turned on, maybe intentionally so the hikers could find their way back to the tent once the winds subsided. The expedition proposed that the group of hikers constructed two bivouac shelters, one of which collapsed, leaving four of the hikers buried with the severe injuries observed.

## Evidence Supporting It

- A Swedish–Russian expedition in 2019 visited the site and concluded that a violent katabatic wind was a plausible explanation.
- Katabatic winds were implicated in a similar 1978 case at Anaris Mountain in Sweden, where eight hikers were killed and one was severely injured, and the topography of the two locations was noted to be similar.
- A sudden katabatic wind would explain why the hikers needed to urgently leave the tent and seek shelter behind the tree line.
- The flashlight left turned on top of the tent is consistent with the hikers intending to return once the wind subsided.
- The theory accounts for the four hikers found in the ravine by proposing that one of two constructed bivouac shelters collapsed, causing their severe injuries.
- The ICRF investigators confirmed that wind speeds on the night of the tragedy were up to hurricane force, 20–30 m/s (45–67 mph; 72–108 km/h).

## Evidence Against It

- The Wikipedia article does not present specific counterarguments to the katabatic wind theory beyond the general arguments raised against avalanche-related explanations, such as the footprints showing a walking pace rather than a panicked flight.
- Katabatic winds are described as somewhat rare events, making their occurrence on that specific night uncertain.

## Expert Opinions

A Swedish–Russian expedition made to the site in 2019 proposed the katabatic wind hypothesis. The investigators noted the topographical similarity between the Dyatlov Pass site and the Anaris Mountain site in Sweden where a similar tragedy occurred in 1978.

## Status

The katabatic wind hypothesis was proposed by a Swedish–Russian expedition in 2019. In February 2019, Russian authorities reopened the investigation and considered a hurricane (consistent with extreme wind events) as one of three possible explanations alongside avalanche and slab avalanche.
