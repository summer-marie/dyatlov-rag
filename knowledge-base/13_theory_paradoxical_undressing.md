# Theory: Paradoxical Undressing

## What It Proposes

International Science Times posited that the hikers' deaths were caused by hypothermia, which can induce a behaviour known as paradoxical undressing in which hypothermic subjects remove their clothes in response to perceived feelings of burning warmth. Under this theory, the inadequate clothing found on the bodies is explained not by a panicked flight from the tent but by the hikers actively removing their own garments as they succumbed to the final stages of hypothermia.

## Evidence Supporting It

- It is undisputed that six of the nine hikers died of hypothermia.
- The hikers were found only partially dressed despite deadly cold temperatures of approximately −25 to −30 °C (−13 to −22 °F) with a storm blowing. Some had only one shoe, while others wore only socks.
- Paradoxical undressing is a documented and well-understood medical phenomenon associated with fatal hypothermia.

## Evidence Against It

- Others in the group appear to have acquired additional clothing from those who had already died, which suggests that they were of a sound enough mind to try to add layers. This rational behaviour is inconsistent with a group-wide descent into hypothermic confusion.
- The theory does not account for the severe internal injuries — major chest fractures and skull damage — suffered by three of the hikers, which hypothermia alone cannot explain.
- The footprints leaving the tent showed individuals walking at a normal pace, suggesting the group was still in control of their faculties when they initially left.

## Expert Opinions

International Science Times proposed this explanation. No specific named expert is cited in the Wikipedia source as endorsing or refuting paradoxical undressing as a primary cause.

## Status

Paradoxical undressing is considered a contributing factor consistent with the deaths of the six hikers confirmed to have died of hypothermia, but it does not account for the full range of evidence, particularly the three hikers who died of physical trauma.
