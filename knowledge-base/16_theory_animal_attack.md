# Theory: Animal Attack

## What It Proposes

Initial speculation suggested that the indigenous Mansi people, reindeer herders in the area, had attacked and killed the group for encroaching upon their lands. A separate line of speculation proposed that the group was attacked and chased by animal wildlife, which would account for why they fled the tent without gathering clothing or equipment.

## Evidence Supporting It

- The severe soft tissue injuries to the faces and heads of the four hikers found in the stream — including Dubinina's missing tongue, eyes, part of the lips, facial tissue and a fragment of skull bone, Zolotaryov's missing eyeballs, and Kolevatov's missing eyebrows — have been cited by some as consistent with animal predation or scavenging.
- The military testing theory (among others) uses scavenging animals specifically to explain Dubinina's injuries.

## Evidence Against It

- Several Mansi were interrogated, but the investigation indicated that the nature of the deaths did not support the hypothesis of a Mansi attack: only the hikers' footprints were visible, and they showed no sign of hand-to-hand struggle.
- Donnie Eichar evaluated the animal wildlife attack theory and deemed it unlikely: there were no animal tracks, and the group likely would not have abandoned the relative security of the tent.
- Vozrozhdenny stated that the fatal injuries of the three bodies could not have been caused by human beings, "because the force of the blows had been too strong and no soft tissue had been damaged." This same reasoning applies against an animal attack producing the internal fractures.
- V. A. Vozrozhdenny, the forensic expert performing the post-mortem examination, judged that the soft tissue facial injuries happened post-mortem because of the location of the bodies in a stream, not from an attack.

## Expert Opinions

V. A. Vozrozhdenny, the forensic expert performing the post-mortem examination, judged that the soft tissue injuries to the faces and heads of the four hikers found in the stream happened post-mortem because of the location of the bodies in running water. Donnie Eichar, who investigated and made a documentary about the incident, evaluated the animal attack theory and deemed it unlikely or discredited.

## Status

The animal attack theory was among the first speculations considered and was evaluated and dismissed by the original 1959 investigation, which found only the hikers' own footprints at the scene. The post-mortem explanation for the soft tissue facial injuries — decomposition and water action from the stream — is the accepted forensic finding.
